Our interest in ticks is at a peak—is it for good reason?

Two data scientists' deep dive into why everyone is talking about ticks this summer.

By Avani Adhikari and Anna Duan

August 31, 2026

After a seemingly neverending Northeast winter, the first touch of the thermostat past 70 felt like freedom. Finally, we could go outside—touch grass, see light not emitted by a computer screen. Or, so we thought. Because while the frost of winter was receding, a new threat was waiting outside.

Anna: While shampooing after a hike, an unexpected bump on my scalp sent a cold shiver down my spine. This was the exact sensation I felt combing my childhood dog after coming from outside. Surely…not? I tugged at it firmly. A brown tick the size of an uncooked lentil with a white spot on its back wriggled on my forefinger. A lonestar tick.

In the following weeks, we found three more ticks on hikes and walks through the woods, and three separate podcasts I follow released episodes about ticks. I had dreams about ticks. It seemed like they were everywhere.

Was this my heightened awareness or was something going on?

An unusual tick summer

Tick populations, and our awareness of them, surge every summer, but this year has felt more intense. According to the CDC, tick bites this year are at a decade high causing the CDC to put a warning earlier this year. [1] Google searches for ticks are also up: compared to last summer, search interest is up 69%, marking an all-time high.

What's interesting is that while searches about ticks and their diseases in general are increasing, the search terms that are growing most rapidly are people looking for alternative explanations about the rise in ticks. Of the fifty fast-growing tick-related queries, the top five are related to conspiracy theories, namely a growing theory that the government is dropping boxes of ticks on farmland.

So why all the commotion around ticks?

Avani: Growing up in Asia, I had never heard of ticks. So when everyone around me started freaking out about some insect, I had no reference. Why even be afraid of ticks anyways?

As it turns out, there are a lot of valid reasons to be afraid of ticks. Ticks are vectors of more than 25 different illness, with different species carrying different strains of bacteria, virus, and parasites that can dehibilitate humans. Lyme disease, the most common tick-borne illness, is a bacterial infection carried by the Deer Tick and Western Black-legged tick. Even when the ticks do not carry pathogens, bites of some species, most notably the lone-star tick, can cause an antibody response which can induce potentially deadly allergies to red meat known as the Alpha-gal syndrome.[2]

The risk for both diseases has skyrocketed in recent years. Between 2020-2023, reported Lyme disease cases increased fourfold.[3] Alpha gal is exploding, too. First recognized in humans only in 2011, an estimated 450,000 people live with it today.[4]

Part of the increase in tick-borne illness is due to these diseases' increased geographic reach. In the last 10 years, multiple ticks species have expanded their territory as climate change and reforestation makes new habitats viable for them.

Lyme disease-carrying deer ticks, historically limited to the Northeast and Midwest, have been expanding South.[5] Similarly, Lone star ticks, endemic to the Southeast, are being found further North and West than before.[6] This increase in tick endemic regions is causing unforeseen second order effects for infected individuals. As medical professionals in these new host regions have never seen such cases before, tickborne illnesses are going undiagnosed and untreated.[7]

Do we fear ticks, or uncertainty?

Ticks are scary not because of what we know about them, but because of what we don't fully know. Despite humans coexisting alongside ticks for millenia, scientific study of its public health impacts is relatively recent. Most of the famous illnesses associated with ticks—lyme disease included—was only discovered in the later half of 20th century.[8] Today, there are far more scientists studying the implications of ticks on our lives, but we are far from knowing what to do about it and the science is constantly changing.

For those suffering from tick-borne illnesses or living in areas where the risk is increasing, this uncertainty can be very distressing. Currently, there is no widespread preventative medicine for tick borne illnesses other than avoiding green areas. As not all tick bites cause disease, the standard CDC guidance is simply to remove the tick, and wait until symptoms emerge before pursuing any treatment.[9].

It is therefore not surprising why many people, confronted with the complicated science of blood-sucking insects and the lack of actionable government guidance, turn to conspiracy theories. After all, when the scientifically recommended advice is simply, “stop going outside”, why wouldn't you believe that nefarious forces are behind it?

This piece had started as an exercise of personal curiosity. Every day that we've worked on this project, we've seen new podcasts, news articles, and research papers published as the science continues to evolve and the crisis deepens. We struggled to piece together the research papers and government guidance and understand what it means for us.

As spatial data people, we wanted to read analyses of where cases are occuring and who they are impacting, and found very few. The more we read, the more questions we had. Who is most impacted by the tick-borne public health crisis? What are the factors contributing to its rapid spread? What are tick-borne diseases and how do you get them? Why is there so little information about ticks in the first place—and why are tick conspiracy theories so popular?

We quickly realized that this research can't be limited to just a few data visualizations or an article. Ticks deserve something bigger than that. In the next few months, we will be unpacking our findings and sharing them so that others who share our concern have a centralized repository of information. There still are more unknowns than known—but at least we can confirm that the government is not dropping boxes of ticks in farms (probably).
